# The ERP frame I built two days of analysis on has essentially no predictive power

Verified 2026-08-27 by the Base-Rate archetype. **Confidence: this is the strongest, most
convergent evidence in the entire knowledge base** — multiple independent researchers, different
methods, same conclusion.

**Comparing earnings yield to bond yield — the "Fed model" — does not forecast equity returns.**

| Study | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Ned Davis Research (2018)** | Fed-model spread **R² = 0.5%** for forward 10-year S&P returns, against **56.3% for plain trailing P/E** and **67.2% for Price/Sales** |
| **Asness, "Fight the Fed Model" (2003)** | "No power to forecast long-term real stock returns." Mechanism is **money illusion** — investors anchor P/E to *nominal* rates, producing in-sample co-movement with no out-of-sample content. Notes it "almost always gives a buy signal" — a sales tool |
| **Estrada (2006, 2009)** | Tested the equilibrium claim (E/P = bond yield) across **20 countries. It held in 2.** |
| **Hulbert (2018, 2020)** | **Adding** the bond-yield term to a plain E/P regression **reduces** predictive power. The term is noise |
| **Salomons (2006)** | E/P–bond-yield correlation was **19% over 1881-2002**, spiking to ~75% only in 1995-2000 and 1921-28 — **a period artifact, not a regularity** |
| **Smithers (2014)** | "The most egregious piece of data mining" in sixty years |

**No study was found showing the Fed model outperforming CAPE or P/E in any market or period.**

## And even the sign is methodology-dependent

**Damodaran's rigorous implied-ERP series shows NO negative-ERP years across 1960-2025**, while a
raw earnings-yield-minus-10-year computation finds negative spreads frequently. **The two
methodologies diverge substantially.**

So "India's ERP is negative" is not a fact about the world — **it is an artifact of one particular
naive construction**, and the more rigorous construction does not produce negative readings at all.

## What DOES have signal — and it points the other way for India

In the five identifiable US episodes, **the outcome split cleanly by the CAUSE of the negative gap,
not by its existence:**

| Cause | Episodes | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **High bond yields**, not expensive equities | **1981-84** | **Equities won big** — roughly +150-200% nominal by 1986-87 as yields mean-reverted down |
| **Rich equity valuations** | 1972-73, 1999-2000 | **Equities lost, sometimes badly** — 1973-74 fell 48%; the S&P took ~13 years to reclaim its 2000 real high |

**Which is India today?**

- **Nifty P/E 20.46 is 12.6% BELOW its own ten-year average of 23.33.** That is not rich valuation.
- **The G-sec is at 6.85% with CPI heading to a projected 5.9% peak.** That is a high nominal bond
  yield.

**India looks structurally more like 1981-84 than like 1999-2000** — the negative gap is coming
from the bond side, not from expensive equities. In the historical sample, that is the case where
equities won.

**Caveat that matters: n = 5.** That is a pattern, not a law, and it is US-only — no usable data
was recovered for Japan, India, Europe or broader EM.

## What I now have to retract

I have spent two days treating "the ERP is negative" as the central bearish fact. The chain of
corrections in one day:

1. It is **pre-tax** — after tax it is roughly zero ([[india-erp-after-tax-is-roughly-zero]])
2. It used the **wrong duration** — duration-matched it is *positive*
   ([[first-principles-caught-four-of-my-errors]])
3. **The metric itself has an R² of 0.5%** and adding the bond term to plain E/P makes forecasts
   *worse*
4. And **the sign is construction-dependent** — Damodaran's method never produces one

**The honest position: the ERP argument should carry almost no weight in the India decision.**
The valuation question should be asked through **plain P/E or CAPE against history** — which
have R² of 56% and 67% — and on that measure India is **12.6% cheap**, not expensive.

## What could NOT be established, and why that matters

The agent was explicit that **the tool constraint was binding, not the absence of history.** With
WebSearch exhausted, five of seven questions returned nothing usable:

- **Profit-to-GDP mean reversion** — and the literature itself is split. Grantham calls profit
  share "the most mean-reverting series in finance"; Goldman argues it is not historically
  mean-reverting. **Unresolved, not settled** — so my "margins are at a peak, they must revert"
  reasoning is an imported prior, not a demonstrated fact.
- **Extreme single-year underperformance** — zero verified instances. Cannot say whether it
  mean-reverts or persists.
- **Foreign-ownership troughs** — n=1, and **the episode has not ended**, so there is no forward
  return to score. It cannot yet be called a contrarian buy or a value trap.
- **Consensus earnings misses** — no year-by-year record recovered. The "India misses every year
  since 2011" claim I supplied **was itself challenged as unverified**.
- **SIP stickiness through drawdowns** — and here the agent found something that undercuts my
  framing: **SIP stoppage ratios (closures ÷ new accounts) ran 300% in Apr 2025, 85% in Dec 2025,
  91% in Jul 2026 and 31% in Aug 2026**, with reports that ~90% of direct-plan SIP investors exit
  within five years. Gross flows can stay positive while churn underneath is severe. **"The SIP bid
  is durable" is an unverified headline narrative, not a demonstrated pattern.**

It also explains the FPI-ownership figures I could not reconcile: the framing kept shifting as the
decline extended — 15-year low, then decade-low, then 15-year, then 14-year, then 17-year.

## The method lesson

**I built a two-day analytical frame on a metric with an R² of 0.5% and never once asked whether it
predicts anything.** The Base-Rate archetype answered in one pass what I had assumed for two days.

**Before building on any metric, ask what its forecasting record is.** Intuitive appeal and
predictive power are unrelated.
