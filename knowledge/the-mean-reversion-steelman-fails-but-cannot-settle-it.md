# The strongest case for India fails on the data — and the data cannot settle it

Having found that the choice is [[the-india-premise-fails-its-own-base-rate|India versus the US]],
the honest next move is to argue the other side as hard as possible. The best available argument for
India is not about India at all: **US exceptionalism is at a record and due to revert.** Tested
three ways. It fails all three, and then the test itself fails, which is the part that matters.

## 1. The concentration argument fails

The standard claim is that US returns are a narrow mega-cap phenomenon at a record share, which must
unwind.

- Cap-weight / equal-weight S&P ratio sits at the **77th percentile** of its own history — elevated,
  **not extreme**.
- Over the trailing year, **equal-weight returned +19.7% against cap-weight's +19.3%.** The broad
  market slightly *out*-performed the concentrated one.

Concentration is not currently increasing, so there is nothing here that has to unwind. This matches
the earlier result that **equal-weight S&P beat cap-weight in rupees over 18 years** (+16.39% vs
+15.27%). The concentration argument does not merely fail to support India — the data points the
other way.

## 2. The mean-reversion argument has real support, and still never flipped the sign

Testing whether five years of US excess return over developed-ex-US predicts the *next* five:

- **Correlation −0.44, R² 0.197.** Genuine negative autocorrelation. Strong outperformance really
  does predict *weaker* outperformance ahead.
- But: after the **top quartile** of US outperformance, the next five years still delivered
  **+4.83%/yr of US excess — positive in 26 of 26 windows.**
- After the **bottom quartile**: +6.88%/yr, **positive in 26 of 26.**

In **102 overlapping five-year windows there is not one** in which the US failed to outperform over
the following five years. Mean reversion here governs the *magnitude* of US outperformance, never
its *sign*. An investor who correctly predicted "US outperformance will shrink" would still have
been wrong to leave.

## 3. Against India specifically there is no mean reversion at all

Same test, S&P-in-INR against the Nifty: **correlation +0.16** — mildly *positive*. Past
outperformance predicts slightly more of it, not less. After the top quartile, the next five years
still ran **+4.27%/yr with the S&P ahead in 25 of 26.**

And the decisive detail for anyone waiting for a stretched gap to close: **the current five-year
reading is +3.61%/yr, at the 26th percentile.** The S&P's advantage over the Nifty is currently in
the *bottom* quartile of its own eighteen-year range. There is no extreme here to revert from. The
"gap is stretched" argument fails on the arithmetic before it reaches the economics.

## And now the part that actually matters

**Every number above is drawn from a window beginning March 2008.** That is 222 months — and the
102 five-year windows overlap so heavily that they contain roughly **four independent
observations**, not 102.

More seriously: the entire sample *is* the post-GFC US bull market. **You cannot test whether a
regime is ending using only data from inside the regime.** A test built on 2008–2026 will conclude
that 2008–2026 continues, because that is the only thing it has ever seen. The three results above
are consistent with US persistence, and they are also exactly what a sample would show one month
before a regime turned.

So the correct statement is narrow and worth stating precisely:

> **Within the only regime we have data for, the mean-reversion case for India is unsupported and
> the current gap is not stretched. That is evidence about the regime, not about whether the regime
> holds.**

What would genuinely test it is out-of-sample: 1970s US underperformance, the 1980s Japan episode,
the 2000s EM decade — periods where developed-ex-US and EM beat the US for a decade at a time.
Those exist and are not in this data. **Until that is run, the base-rate finding should be held as
strong evidence about the last two decades and weak evidence about the next one.**

## What this means for the book

1. The 4.5pp/yr hurdle **stands** — nothing here weakens it, and two of three tests strengthen it.
2. But it should be applied as *"India must justify itself"*, **not** as *"India cannot win"*. The
   distinction is the difference between a hurdle and a prohibition.
3. **Do not use "US concentration must revert" as a reason to hold Indian equities.** It is the most
   commonly offered argument and it is not supported here.
4. The genuine India case has to be built on something this data cannot see: a change in the
   external position that stops the rupee bleeding ~3.5%/yr, or an earnings acceleration that
   survives contact with the nine-year miss record. **Both are testable, neither is yet true.**

**Source:** own computation, `scripts/us_exceptionalism_test.py`, 222 months from March 2008. The window limitation described above is the dominant caveat on every number in this note.
