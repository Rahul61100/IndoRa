# In dollars, India has lost money for two years — and that is what the FII sees

**Computed from our own series** (`scripts/portfolio_risk.py`, Nifty deflated by USDINR),
2026-08-26. **This corrects a framing error I made repeatedly.**

| Window | Nifty in INR | Nifty in USD | Currency drag |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 months | -4.9% | **-9.5%** | -4.6pp |
| 1 year | -1.7% | **-9.9%** | -8.2pp |
| 2 years | -3.2% | **-15.0%** | **-11.8pp** |

Set that against the US over the same year: **S&P +19.2%, Nasdaq +21.6%, Russell 2000 +28.7%**,
all already in dollars.

**A dollar investor has lost roughly 15% in Indian equities over two years while making about 19%
in the US. That is a gap of more than 30 points, and it is the actual decision a global allocator
faced.**

**Why it matters:** I explained FII selling with valuation and narrative. The real explanation is
arithmetic. No amount of "Indian large caps are 12.6% below their 10-year average P/E" competes
with a 30-point relative return gap in the currency the allocator reports in. The FII is not
being irrational or short-termist; the FII is being paid.

It also reframes the domestic bid. SIP flows and a 25-month DII buying streak
([[india-supply-versus-the-sip-bid]]) are rupee flows that do not care about the dollar return.
So the marginal domestic buyer and the marginal foreign seller are looking at genuinely different
assets. That divergence can persist for years — it is not an arbitrage that closes.

**How to apply:**
- **Quote Indian index returns in both currencies, always.** An INR-only figure overstates the
  case to any reader who thinks in dollars.
- FII flows turn when the *dollar* return turns, which needs either an equity move or a rupee
  stabilisation. Track USDINR as a component of the India thesis, not as background.
- The rupee is at 95.4, 8.3% weaker over a year, and RBI's policy rate is on hold with inflation
  rising ([[rate-cut-cycle-is-over]]) — the currency has no obvious catalyst to reverse.
